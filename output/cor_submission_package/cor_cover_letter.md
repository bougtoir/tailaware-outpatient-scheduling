Dear Editors,

I submit "Service-Time Distributional Uncertainty in Outpatient Scheduling: When Simple Interval Optimization Is Enough" for consideration in Computers & Operations Research.

This is a computational study of appointment scheduling under distributional uncertainty. Using a finite-horizon Lindley-type delay recursion evaluated by large-scale Monte Carlo simulation with common random numbers, I hold the service-time mean fixed and vary only the distributional family and tail shape across ten specifications (gamma, Weibull, lognormal, mixture, and Pareto-type designs) and session sizes. The findings are computational in nature: fixed mean-based slots leave 36.7–71.3% of attainable savings unrealized; a single SAA-optimized uniform interval recovers nearly all attainable benefit (maximum relative regret 1.4% versus a nonuniform oracle); and a complexity frontier over block-structured interval vectors shows that the residual benefit of position-dependent schedules concentrates in a few boundary degrees of freedom. Deliberately constructed counterexamples — nonstationary service means, known heterogeneous bookings, and high overtime pricing — delimit exactly where richer designs pay. A misspecification study shows that optimizing under a wrongly assumed family can erase these gains entirely, with under-designed intervals costing about three times as much as over-designed ones.

I believe the work fits the journal's areas of scheduling and decision-making under uncertainty: its contribution is a controlled computational comparison of policy classes — fixed, quantile-based, class-based, SAA-optimized uniform and nonuniform, CVaR-aware, and distributionally robust — evaluated under identical simulation conditions, together with a quantification of the marginal value of schedule complexity. Every number, figure, and table is regenerated by the accompanying open simulation pipeline, and the full code and processed-data package is included for reproducibility.
