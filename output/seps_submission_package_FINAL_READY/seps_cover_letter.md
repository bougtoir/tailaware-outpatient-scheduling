Dear Editors,

I submit "When Does Appointment-Scheduling Complexity Pay? Service-Time
Uncertainty and the Value of Simple Planning Rules" for consideration in
Socio-Economic Planning Sciences.

Clinics face a recurring service-planning problem: appointment intervals
must be committed before demand is realized, and consultation durations
are strongly right-skewed. The practical question is not whether to
schedule — every clinic already does — but how much scheduling
*sophistication* to buy: is a single well-chosen common interval enough,
or do slot-by-slot, risk-aware, or distributionally robust designs earn
their complexity? This paper answers that question with quantitative
planning evidence. Holding the mean service time fixed, I vary only the
distributional family and tail shape across ten specifications (gamma,
Weibull, lognormal, mixture, Pareto-type) and session sizes, using a
finite-horizon Lindley-type delay recursion evaluated by large-scale
Monte Carlo simulation with common random numbers.

The results are directly usable as planning rules. Fixed mean-based
slots — the dominant heuristic — leave 36.7–71.3% of attainable savings
unrealized at session size N = 30 across the ten specifications. Yet a
single SAA-optimized uniform interval recovers nearly all attainable
benefit (maximum relative regret 1.4% versus a nonuniform oracle in the
studied domain): the planning priority is calibrating one number against
a well-estimated service-time distribution, not deploying individualized
machinery. A complexity frontier over block-structured interval vectors
shows that ten of twenty-nine adjustable parameters capture 91–97% of the
uniform-to-full gap, and that the residual value concentrates in a few
boundary positions (first/last three slots close 85–95% of the gap versus
3–9% for interior positions). Deliberately constructed counterexamples —
nonstationary service means (27% regret), known heterogeneous bookings
(32%), and high overtime pricing (4%) — mark the bounded conditions where
richer designs genuinely pay. A misspecification study shows the risk
cuts the other way too: optimizing under a wrongly assumed family can
erase the gains entirely, with under-designed intervals costing about
three times as much as over-designed ones, and moment-fitted designs
remaining unreliable even at 1,000 historical observations when the true
family is heavy-tailed.

I believe this is more than an algorithm comparison: it quantifies the
value of distributional information, the cost of model misspecification,
and the marginal value of decision complexity — the quantities a
service-sector planner must trade off when choosing how elaborate a
scheduling rule to implement. The work speaks to this journal's ongoing
conversation on quantitative planning in the service sector, including
slot-structure design for clinical scheduling (Chakraborty, Muthuraman
and Lawley, 2013), simulation-based healthcare capacity planning (Shoaib
et al., 2023), and scheduling-rule design for hospital staff (Alaouchiche
et al., 2024). The model is deliberately transparent — a single-provider
finite-session system evaluated on a synthetic design space — and the
manuscript states that scope plainly rather than claiming field
validation or monetary outcomes it does not have.
